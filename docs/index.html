Choosing where to build AI data centers as heat waves grow

November 2026 ·
Valmik Nahata, Varun Sinha, Rishab Kolan and Rishil Chintalapati, UC San Diego

AI data centers are approved one site at a time, but heat waves strike many at once. Using ten CMIP6 climate models and 77 planned US campuses, we rank every county as a place to build and show why the fleet's shared heat risk matters for the grid and its regulators.

Appendix: figure notes

Definitions

A place is in extreme heat on a day hotter than its design day, the temperature exceeded on only 1% of days in 1995–2014 in that climate model, close to the condition cooling systems are sized for4. Comparing each model with its own past cancels model biases. A shared heat day is a day when at least a quarter of planned US AI power is in extreme heat at once. A grid region is under region-wide heat when at least half of its area is above its design day.

Figure 1

Four criteria are ranked across counties and averaged. Shared heat with the fleet is how often a county is in extreme heat on the fleet's shared heat days; today's siting tools weigh power, water, land and cooling cost but not this12,13. Local extreme heat counts days above the county's design day. Cooling burden counts days above 35°C, which drive cooling energy and water use14,15. Grid concentration favours grid regions hosting less of the planned fleet. Climate fields are on a 1° grid, so county values inherit the roughly 100 km resolution of the models. The ten best areas are chosen greedily at least 500 km apart; DOE's 16 candidate sites come from its 2025 request for information9.

Figure 2

For each planned site, the map shows how often every county is in extreme heat on the days that site is, pooled over ten models and 20 summers. Heat waves span hundreds of kilometres and push several grids to their limits on the same afternoons5, so the footprint of one large campus routinely covers many others.

Figure 3

The longest run of shared heat days in the 2050s ensemble (moderate emissions), from one model's simulated summer. It is one plausible summer, not a forecast; it shows how a single season moves through the fleet region by region.

Figure 4

Each period's median summer is drawn as 153 days with its shared heat days placed on the most likely dates. Plotting each model's shared heat days against the global warming it simulates gives about 12 days at 1.5°C, 21 at 2°C and 38 at 3°C; global warming levels add 0.85°C to each model's warming since 1995–201423.

Figure 5

Regions border each other when any of their states share a border: West borders ERCOT, SPP and MISO; ERCOT borders West, SPP and MISO; SPP borders West, ERCOT and MISO; MISO borders West, ERCOT, SPP, PJM and the Southeast; the Southeast borders MISO and PJM; PJM borders MISO, the Southeast and NYISO; NYISO borders PJM (ISO New England is not a study region). Counts pool May to September days across ten models and 20 summers per period. NERC's 2024 Interregional Transfer Capability Study found import capability ranging from 1% to 92% of peak load across regions29.

Figure 6

Each measure's coverage is approximated from its scope: state rules by the state of each planned site, FERC orders by the four FERC-regulated markets, Texas SB 6 by ERCOT, DOE's May 2026 emergency order by PJM, and the executive order and NERC standards by the whole fleet; DOE's federal sites host no planned capacity in Epoch AI's records yet. For the heat season, each date from May 1 to September 30 is scored by the share of the 200 model summers in each climate (ten CMIP6 models, 20 years each) in which at least a quarter of the planned power a measure covers is above its sites' design days at once, the same threshold as the fleet's shared heat days, smoothed over five days; the day counts beside each strip are the sum, the expected number of such days a summer. Measures were coded from the cited legal and news summaries for what each addresses: federal permitting and DOE's site program speed approval7,8; FERC's June 2026 show-cause orders and state tariffs set connection terms10,25,26; Texas SB 6 and FERC's July 2026 order on NERC standards cover grid emergencies11,28. DOE's May 2026 emergency order moved PJM data centers onto backup generation during a heat wave27, the only measure touching shared heat, and only during the event. The coding is not a full legal analysis.

Data and method

Primary dataset: CMIP6, one of the three course datasets. Every heat statistic on this page comes from it. Daily maximum temperature from ten CMIP6 models2 (; first member each) via the public cloud archive3, for 1995–2014 and for 2041–2060 and 2081–2100 under SSP2-4.5 (moderate) and SSP5-8.5 (high emissions). Data center locations, planned power and construction timelines are from Epoch AI1 (CC BY 4.0); we use them only to decide where to sample the CMIP6 fields and how to weight each site, not as a source of climate data. County and state outlines are from the US Census via us-atlas. Grid regions and their regulators are approximated by states, and DOE site coordinates beyond the four selected sites are approximate. Results hold for 98th to 99.5th percentile thresholds and 25% to 50% fleet shares. Heat stands in for grid stress; it is not a record of emergencies.

Write-up

Design rationale

Motivation. AI data centers are approved one site at a time, but a heat wave can push many of them, and the grids they share, to their limits on the same days. We wanted a page that answers a planner's question, where should the next data centers go, using the course's CMIP6 data, and that shows why the answer depends on the fleet as a whole rather than on single sites.

Data transformations. From daily maximum temperature in ten CMIP6 models we compute each place's design day (its own 1995–2014 99th percentile, so model biases cancel), flag days above it as extreme heat, and aggregate: across sites, weighted by planned power, into shared heat days; across a grid region's area into region-wide heat; across counties into percentile ranks for siting risk; and across models into medians. Each figure's caption and the appendix state which transformation it shows.

Colour palette. All risk uses one sequential palette, magma, which is perceptually uniform and readable in greyscale, running from pale (low risk) to dark (high risk). Grey carries context such as unselected options, and a single mid-magma accent marks the one thing to look at in a figure. We avoided blue and green and diverging palettes, because every measure here runs one way, from less heat to more.

Story and order. The article opens with its answer, a county siting map, and then works backwards through the evidence: where the heat reaches (Figure 2), what one summer looks like (Figure 3), how often this happens as the planet warms (Figure 4), what it means for the grid (Figure 5) and who has the power to act (Figure 6). Each figure answers one question, and its lead-in sentence states that answer before the reader looks at the chart.

Consistent visual language. All risk uses one perceptually uniform colour scale (magma, pale for low and dark for high), so a dark county in Figure 1 and a dark ribbon in Figure 5 mean the same thing. Context is grey, emphasis is a single accent, and data centers are always squares. We dropped an early palette of reds and blues, and a later charcoal one, because readers had to relearn the encoding in each figure.

Figure 1, siting explorer. A county choropleth fits the decision being made (local zoning and permitting happen at county level) and lets the four criteria be combined into one rank. Toggling criteria and climates re-ranks in place, a search box finds any county, and clicking zooms to its state with the five best counties numbered. We tried a smooth contour surface and hexagons first; counties were more legible and matched existing siting tools.

Figure 2, heat reach. The same county geometry shows how far one site's extreme heat extends. A tour of the largest campuses animates the footprint growing and shifting; clicking any site selects it. Earlier versions showed the fleet as bubbles, a ridgeline relief, dot maps, per-site build-out strips and a unit histogram; all described where the fleet is but not why its location matters, which this map does directly.

Figure 3, one simulated summer. An animated county map is paired with a site-by-day grid so that the same moment can be read spatially and across the whole fleet. Playback and a scrubbable timeline let readers stop on the worst day.

Figure 4, typical summers. Showing each period's median summer as 153 days turns an abstract count into a share of the season that anyone can read. Scatter plots against global warming, unit charts of all 1,000 simulated summers and stacked bands were each accurate but harder to interpret.

Figure 5, neighbouring grids. A grid region in trouble imports power from its neighbours, so the figure asks one question per region: on its worst days, are its neighbours in trouble too? Small multiples give each of the seven grid regions the same small chart, placed roughly where the region sits, so readers can compare regions at a glance. Each column splits a period's region-wide heat days by how many bordering regions are also under region-wide heat, with light to dark meaning more neighbours, matching the risk scale used throughout. The figure is deliberately static. Earlier versions (a calendar of regional heat, a network map, a chord diagram, a dumbbell chart, a siting simulator, a placement game, exceedance curves and an animated unit chart) were either harder to read or repeated Figures 2 to 4.

Figure 6, governance. A table of measures against the questions they address makes the gap visible as an empty column. Selecting a measure links the table to the climate data in the same way the county explorer works: the heat season below redraws for the load that measure covers, one strip per climate on a shared calendar, so readers see both how many more shared heat days arrive and how the season stretches from midsummer into June and September.

Interaction choices. Every figure animates once when it scrolls into view and can be replayed or explored by hover and click. Tooltips carry the detail so that the charts stay free of labels and numbers. One climate control, inside Figure 1, also drives Figure 2.
